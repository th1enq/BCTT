# Scientific Presentations: Abstracts, Talks, Slides, Posters, Q&A

## Conference abstracts

Three audiences at once: organizers (accept? oral or poster?), attendees
(come to your talk?), and the online archive (permanent record). Best
abstracts are: **new, interesting** (conference abstracts aren't peer
reviewed — you may be bolder than in a journal), **focused** (a 10-minute
talk carries 1–2 points), **informative** (specific results — never "results
will be discussed"; though you may hold back the punchline), understandable
to a broader audience, enticingly titled, and well written. Avoid future
tense. Don't submit "fabstracts" for work not yet begun. Write days before
the deadline, in a word processor (never directly into the web form), and
have colleagues read it. Request a poster when the topic is intricate,
niche, or you prefer one-on-one discussion — posters are not a consolation
prize. Extended abstracts: not peer reviewed (list separately on a CV); a
useful rough draft of the eventual paper; mimic a prior template.

## Talks ≠ spoken papers

Presentations differ from writing: they must be focused (details wait for
Q&A or the paper); the audience is captive, paced by you, one-shot; feedback
is real-time; you may be provocative and fresh. Exploit those differences.

- **Five minutes per substantive point** (Doswell). A 10-minute talk = two
  points max. Build the whole talk around them; scrutinize every slide's
  contribution.
- Know why you're talking (inform / persuade / inspire / honor) and to whom.
  Don't overshoot or undershoot the audience; ask organizers beforehand.
  Hostile audience → noncontroversial evidence first, conclusion last.
- **Baud-rate management:** overload and the audience receives nothing;
  underload and they daydream. Words and visuals must complement, not
  duplicate: text-heavy slides split attention; reading slides verbatim is
  redundant (they read faster than you speak).
- **Dive-and-surface for diverse audiences** (Alley): start where everyone
  can follow, dive deep for specialists, and *surface* at each topic's end
  and at the close to regather the whole room. Repeat per topic; also works
  for multi-discipline audiences (touch each group's concerns in turn). A
  well-presented general talk beats a poorly presented specialist talk.

## Building slides

- **Storyboard before opening the software** — sticky notes or index cards;
  organize the message first (the hard part), style later (the fun part).
  Write speaker notes for each planned slide.
- **One point per slide**; no discernible point → delete the slide. Never
  state the obvious ("hail is an important forecast problem") — give the
  statistics that prove it.
- **Openers:** title slide passes quickly. **Kill the "Outline of my talk"
  slide** (Introduction–Data–Methods–Conclusions is information-free and
  loses the room). Open with motivation, purpose, or evidence that
  contradicts expectations ("If not by advection, how do strong cold fronts
  happen in Utah?"). No literature-review slides; frame debates around
  concepts, not paper lists.
- **Closers:** one well-worded conclusion slide with real results (not "the
  model was capable of reproducing X"); a conceptual schematic works
  beautifully. Leave it up through Q&A. Nothing after it: no "Questions?",
  no "Thank you" slide, no reference list; "Future work" only if genuinely
  exciting.
- **Headlines beat titles:** a full-sentence headline stating the slide's
  point ("Zonal wind variations, not heating, cause Kelvin wave
  amplification" — not "Results"). Upper-left, 28–40 pt, left-justified,
  ≤ 2 lines, active voice. It forces you to define the point and rescues
  listeners who missed your words.
- **Design:** consistent look throughout (a deliberate mid-talk style change
  signals a turn); skip stock templates, gaudy branded backgrounds, and
  per-slide logos/footers; simple dark background + light text (dark
  blue/green/purple with white/yellow) projects well — but graphs read best
  dark-on-white; never yellow/light-green/light-blue on white; no red on
  blue; sans serif ≥ 18 pt; NO LONG CAPITALS; left-justify only.
- **Words:** fewer for lay audiences; more complete phrases when presenting
  in a language foreign to you or the audience. Don't use slides as your
  teleprompter — rehearse. Bullets: question each one (they imply equal
  weight); ≤ 4 items; parallel phrases, not sentences; meaningful order.
  Minimize equations (define variables, annotate physical meaning);
  graphics over words.
- **Graphics:** publication figures rarely work on screen — enlarge labels,
  simplify; put caption-like info on the slide (rescues daydreamers);
  acknowledge downloaded graphics; real photos beat clip art for emotional
  connection; ≤ ~7 items per slide; empty space is power.
- **Transitions:** build complex diagrams step-by-step (cloud outline →
  precipitation → cold pool → airflow → cells: "busy but not
  inaccessible"); otherwise avoid item-by-item bullet reveals (clicks cost
  time and chain you to the keyboard); use fast "appear" transitions, never
  random/garish ones.
- Portability: bring your own laptop when possible; standard fonts; embed
  graphics; keep linked media in one folder; equations as images; consider
  a PDF fallback; **test the transferred file thoroughly**.

## Delivering

- **Rehearse out loud, standing** — mental review is not rehearsal. Shorter
  talk = more rehearsal (first conference talk: 5–10 runs). Script the first
  30 seconds and memorize them for momentum. If a section keeps stumbling,
  script it — writing it out fixes it in memory. Reread the underlying
  paper before presenting old work.
- Before the session: arrive early; upload and *test* (animations, videos);
  kill screensavers and popups; test clicker range and the laser button;
  check projector colors; mic on your centerline; water; bathroom.
- Openings: don't repeat the introduction the chair just gave; start
  comfortably brisk (your opening pace sets the talk's pace); expect
  attention to sag ~20 minutes into an hour talk — break rhythm with a
  story, question, quick video, chalkboard moment, or blank screen ("b" in
  PowerPoint).
- Keep slides moving (5 minutes on one slide is taxing); compress runs of
  similar slides; skip material a previous speaker covered — it shows
  respect and flexibility.
- Finish by surfacing to the big picture — never leave the audience in the
  depths. End with "Thank you" (the applause cue), not "that's all I have".
- Delivery: be natural, sincere, professionally enthusiastic; smile; you are
  the expert — no self-deprecation. Speak slowly and clearly (more so with
  ESL listeners); vary intonation; stand; kill filler (um, you know) with
  silent pauses; still hands; a stick/hand beats a laser (lasers amplify
  nervous shake; turn it off after pointing).
- **Eye contact:** face the audience, not the screen; one person for 1–2
  seconds, then move; > 50% of the time overall, ~90% at open and close.
- **Never exceed your time.** One slide per minute (slower for non-native
  audiences); track time from the podium; better to end early — leave them
  wanting more (the 40-minute band beat the 80-minute band).
- Never say: "I'll finish early", "I didn't get much done on this", "sorry
  this is preliminary" (conferences exist for work in progress), "I know
  you're hungry", "you won't be able to see this", "I'll speed up",
  "just one more graph".

## Q&A

Predict the weaknesses of your own talk; have colleagues fire hard questions
in rehearsal; stash backup slides *after* the conclusion slide for expected
questions. Ask "What questions do you have?" (more inviting than "Any
questions?"); step toward the audience; wait out the silence patiently.
Listen to the whole question; answer the question asked, not the one you
wished for; confirm ("If I understand, you're asking…?" — also buys
thinking time). "I don't know" honestly beats handwaving; admit mistakes
gladly. Egotists: "Do you have a question, or are you making a statement?"
Monopolizers: "Let's continue after the session." Hostile questioner:
answer to the whole room, break eye contact with the aggressor. Have a
friend write the questions down — they improve the next talk and the paper.
No questions ≠ failure (hunger, fatigue, culture). Linger after the session.

## Posters

Advertising logic: the unexpected conclusion attracts; configuration details
don't. Posters demand even more focus than talks.

- **Two designs:** *self-discovery* (works without you: intro box spanning
  the top posing the central question; each results box = headline + one
  graphic + 1–2 bullets) and *interactive* (minimal words, big graphics,
  you drive the conversation). Most posters blend both.
- **One-minute synopsis method** (Lakshmanan): write the 1-minute version of
  the research → structure the poster around it → prepare a 5-minute
  version → anything beyond the 5-minute version stays off the poster.
- Layout: short punchy title at 72+ pt (readable at 5 m); body 36+ pt
  (1–2 m); section heads 54+ pt; ≥ 50% figures/photos; generous white
  space; 2–3 columns with boxes/numbers/arrows guiding reading order;
  conclusions prominent (top beats bottom-right) in a contrasting box.
  First drafts are always too wordy: cut and cut and cut.
- Avoid: background photos/patterns, long text blocks, small graphs,
  complex tables, equations, pixelated images, chartjunk, reference lists
  (put them in the handout).
- Paper: dark type on light background; remake publication figures for
  distance viewing; add your photo, email, and web page; proof a reduced
  test print (colors and typos); know the venue's poster dimensions.
- At the session: bring extended abstracts, business cards, a letter-size
  copy; offer "Could I walk you through my poster?"; hook with the 1-minute
  synopsis, expand to 5 minutes for the hooked; include latecomers joining
  mid-spiel; interactive props (flip-windows, laptop animation, things to
  touch) work; if traffic is thin, go fishing in the aisles — and bring
  back a big fish.

## Contingencies and nerves

- Travel: fly a day early; presentation never in checked luggage (laptop +
  USB + cloud); poster tube.
- Foreign audiences: slow down (> 1 min/slide); fuller sentences on slides
  and repeat more of them aloud; simpler words, no idioms; more eye contact.
- Nervousness is cured mainly by **preparation**: rehearse until
  comfortable; script the open; visualize success; exercise off the
  adrenaline; skip the caffeine; comfortable confidence-boosting clothes.
  Remember: audiences are jaded and *want* you to succeed; anxiety peaks
  just before and during the first slides, then fades; nobody can see your
  heartbeat — don't tell them you're nervous.
- When equipment fails: breathe; stay cool and even amused; never blame
  anyone; let the moderator fix it; switch to a backup plan (tell a story,
  survey the audience, use the chalkboard, walk the laptop around). Prepare
  a comeback to recapture attention afterward.

## Workplace and media (brief)

- **Memos:** shortness is the #1 virtue (the higher it travels, the shorter
  it must be); only what the audience needs; bullets; a concrete decision
  point with options and the cost of inaction; dates and deadlines on page
  one.
- **Email:** subject line = a title (accurate, concise, compelling); point
  first; bullets; explicit call to action with a deadline; draft important
  emails, sleep on them, read aloud, then send; anything sent can be
  forwarded.
- **Meetings:** first ask if a meeting is needed at all; agenda in advance;
  start/end on time; control tangents; close with action items, owners,
  deadlines.
- **Media interviews:** pre-interview the reporter (outlet, audience, topic,
  live or taped, who else); prepare ≤ 3 talking points as 30-second sound
  bites; rehearse. Never assume the reporter knows the field; repeat the
  question inside your answer; don't fill silences (a classic trap); stay
  inside your expertise ("I don't know, but I'll find out" is always fine);
  no jargon; nothing is ever off the record; never "no comment"; "anything
  to add?" = repeat your key message.
