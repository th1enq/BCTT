# Structuring a Scientific Paper

## Before writing: is it publishable?

Publishability = quality of science × quality of presentation. Neither can
rescue the other. Ask before drafting:

1. Have you asked a good scientific question? ("Sacred cows make the best
   hamburger" — papers that challenge accepted beliefs are the most powerful.)
2. Is something original — a new technique, or a new explanation? State the
   purpose and the original contribution explicitly.
3. Are the conclusions supported by evidence? Speculation is dessert: allowed
   only after a full dinner of evidence, never the whole meal.
4. Is it substantial enough (a "least publishable unit")?
5. Does it have current relevance or future impact? Incremental advances are
   fine — most science is incremental (Kuhn).

Research is rarely bulletproof. Be honest about limitations and use
discipline-standard assumptions; do not let every limitation defeat you.

Choose the target journal early (topic fit, actual vs potential readership,
open access, rejection rate, speed, page charges, color/animation support).
Format for that journal from the start. If unsure whether a journal wants
your paper, email the editor.

## Nonlinear reading — the key structural fact

Readers jump: title → abstract → figures → conclusions → (maybe) the rest.
A Westinghouse study: 100% of managers read the executive summary, 60% the
introduction, 50% the conclusion, 15% the body. Therefore put the
strongest, most polished writing in the parts most people read.

## Title

Five characteristics (trade-offs among them are the author's call):
**informative, accurate, clear, concise, attention-commanding.**

- Draft a working title early for focus; re-check it against the final paper.
- Put the most important words first or last. Don't waste the opening on
  "The/An" or empty words like "study of / investigation of"
  ("An observational study of X" → "Observations of X").
- Include searchable keywords; use common word order ("potential vorticity
  inversion", not "the inversion of potential vorticity").
- Avoid ambiguous acronyms and chemical formulas (spell out CO2 → carbon
  dioxide) — they hurt search and confuse outsiders.
- "Using" titles: ~80% contain misplaced modifiers. "Reexamination of the
  1979 storm using current models" (the storm uses models?) → "Using current
  models to reexamine the 1979 storm."
- Assertive-sentence titles ("Antarctic ice is melting twice as fast…") are
  risky: if later disproven, the wrong title lives forever. Question titles
  intrigue but hurt search — use once or twice in a career.
- Colon titles move the punchy part forward but tend to run long.
- Avoid multipart papers (Part I/II): harder to write and review; Part II may
  never appear. Write standalone papers; if unavoidable, submit parts
  together.

## Abstract

Four contents (Day & Gastel): objectives and scope; methods; summary of
results; principal conclusions. Usually ≤ ~250 words.

- Dense and specific. Ban vague filler ("Differences between two models are
  examined and discussed").
- No citations, no figure/table references, no undefined abbreviations —
  abstracts travel alone in databases.
- Usually written/finalized last; always re-checked against the final text.
- Minimal background; focus on results.

## Introduction (three components, Booth et al.)

1. **Contextualizing background** — ground the reader in familiar material.
   Personal motivation for the problem is underused and welcome.
2. **Problem statement — the hook.** Like a film, a paper engages through
   conflict: a paradox, error, inconsistency in the literature, a knowledge
   gap, or a misunderstanding. Establish common ground, then disrupt it.
3. **Response to the problem** — promise the solution; state the purpose
   explicitly: "The purpose of this paper is to …". Also state the scope
   (what the paper will *not* do) to prevent inflated expectations.

Avoid the "resounding banality" opening ("Tornadoes cause much death and
destruction") unless you will contradict it. Don't give away all results —
leave the reader a reason to continue. The final organization paragraph
("Section 2 …") is better written with context for *why* the paper is
organized that way, not as a bare list. It's fine to write the introduction
after the body.

## Literature synthesis (not "review")

Synthesize and critique; don't recite greatest hits. Like a good box set, it
includes dead-end directions, forgotten gems, unpublished ideas, and open
questions. Four failure modes reviewers flag:

1. **Coverage wrong** — too little (only your own group; "temporal myopia"
   citing only the last decade; ignoring sources you disagree with — discuss
   and rebut them instead) or too much (a bloated tour that exhausts readers).
2. **"Just the facts"** — lists of references without interpretation.
   Describe the *topic*, not the papers; organize thematically or
   chronologically into a narrative.
3. **Significance never argued.**
4. **The grocery list** — "Dunn (1983) showed this. Carpenter (1993) did
   that." No context, no organization.

Avoid unquantifiable claims: "Very little research has investigated X" →
"Compared to the United States, less research on X has been done in Europe
in the last 50 years." The synthesis may live in the introduction, its own
section, or be woven through the paper — put literature where the reader
needs it (e.g., comparisons in the discussion).

## Data and methods

Standard of completeness: a reader could duplicate the study. Incomplete or
wrong methods, once published, get cited for years. No need to cite Excel or
MATLAB unless the specific computation matters. Prefer "methods" over
"methodology" (the study of methods). May be merged into other sections when
brief.

## Results

The meat. Open with the big picture, then most obvious → least obvious.
Select ruthlessly: "the fool collects facts; the wise man selects them" —
even good results that don't advance the story get cut (save them for
another paper). Negative results deserve reporting (often one sentence);
they save others years of dead ends.

## Discussion (optional section)

For: theory and generalizations beyond the results; outliers and exceptions;
alternative interpretations; unresolved issues; comparison with literature;
answering the questions the introduction raised; implications and
significance; limitations; explicitly labeled speculation ("I speculate
that…"). Do not restate results — interpret them. Separating speculation
into a discussion section prevents reviews scolding you for mixing results
and speculation.

## Conclusions / summary / conclusion

"Where this work leaves us (summary) and where it leads us (conclusions)."
Read by both linear and nonlinear readers — polish it like the abstract.
Prose beats bullet lists (lets you integrate evidence). No new material.
Future work: skip the limp "more cases should be studied"; instead offer
specific objectives or testable hypotheses. Avoid promising "will be
reported in future work" (it may never appear). End forcefully.

## Other parts

- **Cover page:** title; authors + affiliations (use a consistent full name
  for your whole career); document type/target journal/status; date of last
  revision; corresponding author contact.
- **Keywords:** imagine what a searcher would type; specific but not
  hyper-narrow; not words already in the title.
- **Acknowledgments:** reviewers (including anonymous), those who gave
  methods/data/software help, funders with grant numbers, disclaimers. Write
  "I thank…", not "I would like to thank…". When in doubt, include the
  person.
- **Appendices:** self-contained derivations/surveys; must be referenced in
  the text; question whether each is truly needed (not a dumping ground).

## Alternative organizations

The IMRaD skeleton flexes: literature can fold into the introduction; a
two-task paper may repeat data/methods+results per task; results and
discussion may merge per task. Reorganize when you keep cross-referencing
figures or text far forward/backward — that's the symptom of wrong order.

## Getting started (motivation, outline, first draft)

- Writing is a job, not inspiration-driven: schedule it, start before the
  research is finished (methods and data sections first — they're factual
  and easy), set chapter-level deadlines, and reward milestones.
- Blocked? Write stream-of-consciousness; skip stuck spots with "BLAH BLAH";
  put both word choices in parentheses to pick later; work on figures or
  references instead; stop mid-flow ("leave water in the well" — Hemingway)
  so tomorrow starts easily.
- Nutshell first: one sentence stating the paper's purpose. Brainstorm ≥90
  minutes past the point of exhaustion, then group themes.
- **Lay out draft figures on a table before writing.** The figure sequence
  reveals the story, exposes unnecessary figures, and shows gaps needing new
  figures.
- Keep an `outtakes` file for deleted text — easier emotionally, and
  sometimes seeds the next paper. Never fall in love with your own text.
