# Style: Paragraphs, Sentences, Words

Paragraphs are built for **unity + coherence**; sentences for
**concision + precision**; words for exactness. Work in that order.

## Accessible writing (reader-first habits)

- Demonstrate points with specific examples; every sentence must do work
  (evidence, citation, hypothesis, speculation). Obvious statements need
  quantitative specifics to earn their place.
- Assume the audience knows less than you; err toward slightly more
  background. Define jargon and assumptions.
- Justify assumptions up front, most plausible → least; anticipate rebuttals
  without apology.
- State limitations and alternative explanations yourself — it *raises*
  credibility; astute readers see them anyway. Play devil's advocate against
  your own argument.
- For a skeptical audience: all evidence first, controversial conclusion
  last, so readers arrive at it with you.
- Argument order: **data → results → interpretation → inference →
  speculation.** Leading with speculation conditions readers to distrust
  everything after.

## Paragraphs

- One theme per paragraph, defined by a **topic sentence** (usually first;
  occasionally last for emphasis). Multiple themes → split, broaden the topic
  sentence, or delete. Writing all topic sentences at the outline stage
  checks the logical flow early.
- **Topic position / stress position** (Gopen & Swan): start of a sentence
  carries old information (links backward, comforts the reader); end carries
  the new information to emphasize (readers naturally stress endings). Chain
  sentences: the stress of one becomes the topic of the next.
- Think of directions for a driver: the good version repeats landmarks
  between steps ("just beyond the coliseum…"), so a lost reader can recover.
- Three coherence devices:
  1. **Lucid repetition** of key words/phrases (links must be obvious;
     repeated nouns beat pronouns more often than writers expect).
  2. **Enumeration**: First, Second, Third (never Firstly); one paragraph
     per item if items run long; or a numbered list/table.
  3. **Transition words** signaling the relation — a handful transforms a
     list of observations into an argument. Categories: sequence
     (furthermore, moreover, in addition), contrast (whereas, in contrast,
     nevertheless, conversely, but, alternatively), example (for example,
     specifically, in particular, such as), cause (therefore, thus,
     consequently, because), time (meanwhile, thereafter, eventually),
     emphasis (indeed, in fact, surprisingly), conclusion (finally, in
     summary, simply put).
- Between paragraphs: same mechanics at larger scale. **Test: read only the
  first 1–2 sentences and last sentence of each paragraph — the story should
  still hold.**
- Length: 4–8 sentences typical; avoid single-sentence paragraphs; break up
  page-long blocks. Vary sentence length and rhythm.
- Sections: ≥2 subsections per level; introductory text between a heading
  and its first subheading; heading titles parallel in form (all verb
  phrases or all noun phrases); before submitting, extract all headings as
  an outline and check the logic.

## Sentences

### Active vs passive voice

Passive = form of "to be" + past participle. Three cures:
1. Make the actor the subject: "Gamma distributions have been used to model
   drop sizes" → "Drop sizes are commonly modeled with gamma distributions."
2. Delete part of the verb: "are perceived to be an important benefit" →
   "are an important benefit."
3. Pick a different verb: "A snowband was initiated" → "A snowband formed."

Use **active** to: emphasize the subject or the scientists' decisions
("we chose option B"; "I speculate that…" — never "It is speculated that…"),
describe figures, write topic sentences, avoid "There are / It has been
shown that". Use **passive** when the agent is unknown/irrelevant, to
emphasize the object, in methods and abstracts, for variety, or to keep the
paragraph's key word in topic/stress position (coherence beats voice).

First person: acceptable in moderation in the body (not the abstract, at
most journals); soften a bold opening "I" with an introductory phrase.
Avoid "the author" (stilted). Single-author "we" (the nosism) is contested.

### Strong verbs

Avoid a diet of is/are/has/have/occur/exist/observed. "Brevity is a
by-product of vigor." Un-nominalize:

| Draft | Improved |
|---|---|
| perform a comparison | compare |
| acts to dry out | dries out |
| is used to denote | denotes |
| was found to be | was |
| makes a measurement | measures |
| provide information | inform |

"An environment favorable for X is the result of Y" →
"Y creates an environment favorable for X."

### Subject–verb distance

Keep them close; words between subject and verb read as unimportant and
leave the reader suspended. "Cyclones with two or more warm-front-like
zones over the central US during 1982–1989 were examined" → "Cyclones over
the central US during 1982–1989 were examined for two or more
warm-front-like zones."

### Tense

- Scientific fact: present ("Ice pellets **are** frozen raindrops").
- Past events: past ("23 cm of snow **fell**").
- Figures/tables/calculations: present ("Table 3 **shows**").
- Started in past, continues: present perfect ("the model **has been
  developed**").
- Pointing ahead in the paper: present beats future ("Section 3
  **discusses**").
- Your own and others' completed work: most use past tense; some argue for
  present. Either way, **be consistent throughout.**

### Parallel structure

In science, repeating structures is a virtue (change one variable at a
time). Items joined by conjunctions take the same form. "The cyclonic path
is represented by trajectories 21–23, whereas trajectories 24–25 resemble
the anticyclonic path" → "Trajectories 21–23 resemble the cyclonic path…,
whereas trajectories 24–25 resemble the anticyclonic path…"

### Comparisons

- Complete them: "was much smaller" — than what?
- With "than", compare like with like: "The static energy of subcloud air is
  higher than the cloudy air" → "…higher than **that of** the cloudy air."
- "Both" is ambiguous (individually vs summed) — rewrite.
- Parenthetical contrasts "When T increases (decreases), RH decreases
  (increases)" → write both sentences out, or "T and RH are inversely
  related."
- former/latter forces re-reading → repeat the nouns.

### Negatives

Readers must parse then invert — slow and error-prone. "Did not prove
conclusive" → "was inconclusive". Never stack negatives
(not…unless…not).

### Misplaced modifiers

Modifiers sit next to what they modify; sentence-opening phrases are the
danger zone. "Inside the tornado, the model results show a decline in wind
speed" (the model is inside the tornado?) → "The model results show a rapid
decline in wind speed inside the tornado."

### Rhythm

Read aloud. If you must read a sentence twice, readers need three passes.
Minimize visual quicksand: dense parentheses, symbols, ALL-CAPS acronyms,
equation-heavy stretches without prose. Footnotes are not a substitute for a
good transition — cut or integrate them.

## Words

### Concision

- No $10 words for 10-cent jobs: utilize→use, initiate→start, perform→do,
  facilitate→cause.
- Kill redundant pairs: (absolutely) essential, (already) existing, (end)
  result, smaller (in size), (model) simulation, mix (together), (time)
  evolution, variety of (different), (very) unique, period (of time),
  (completely) eliminate, first (began).
- Replace bloat (full table in `word-usage.md`): due to the fact
  that→because; in order to→to; it should be noted that→(delete); was
  observed to→was; in the vicinity of→near.
- "It is…" phrases add length AND hide the agent ("It has been hypothesized
  that…" — by whom?). Rewrite with the citation or first person.
- Describe the science, not the figure: "Figure 5 shows plots of X, showing
  that Y" → "Y (Fig. 5)."

### Precision

- Check denotation vs connotation: "state" = declare definitively (≠ say);
  "claim" implies distrust; "significant" implies statistical testing.
- Jargon: fine between experts, defined or replaced otherwise. When synonyms
  coexist (gravity current / density current), mention both once, then pick
  one for the whole paper. Don't coin new terms unless necessary; if you
  must: reuse existing terminology, follow conventions, be accurate and
  appealing, and get it right the first time (published terms are nearly
  impossible to retract).
- Unclear pronouns: isolated "this" and "it" are suspects — search for them
  during revision and attach a noun ("This **range-dependent
  underestimation** means…").
- Keep explicit "that"/"where" — omitting them hurts comprehension,
  especially for ESL readers.
- Weak nouns to interrogate: ability, activity, analysis, approach, case,
  concept, context, dynamics, effect, environment, factor, forcing,
  influence, interaction, issue, level, manner, nature, process,
  relationship, role, sense, situation, system, thing. "A variety of factors
  appear to play a role in why X" → "X occurred for the following three
  reasons…". If a weak word is needed, define it precisely at first use.
- Empty modifiers to cut: very, quite, actually, basically, certainly,
  clearly, obviously, of course, interesting (explain WHY it is
  interesting), important, various, kind of, still, feel (science runs on
  facts, not feelings).
- Time words: "recently"/"now" → "as of June 2006" / "at the time of this
  writing (March 2008)".
- No braggadocio: avoid first/novel/pioneering for your own work; absolutes
  (always, never, best) invite counterexamples; superlatives demand
  quantitative evidence; "numerous papers" → give the citations.

### Proper form

- Abbreviations: define at first use — Full Name (ABBR) — then use the
  abbreviation consistently. Many are unnecessary: if used only a few times,
  spell it out; or substitute a short generic ("the model", "this new
  scheme"). Never invent citation acronyms like "JS06".
- Numbers: measurements/decimals in numerals; ≤ ten spelled out (unless in a
  number list); spell out any number that starts a sentence (better: don't
  start with one); SI units (non-SI first with SI in parentheses).
- Statistics: report test name, statistic, degrees of freedom, p-value
  (p < 0.001 suffices when tiny); check independence assumptions.
- Adjective–noun agreement: "Puppies are warm, not temperatures" —
  temperatures are high/low; velocities large/small; qualitative adjectives
  belong to physical objects.
- No contractions, clichés, colloquialisms, anthropomorphism.

### Bias-free writing

- Gender: neutral nouns (chair, humanity); pluralize to "they"; rewrite to
  drop the pronoun; never assume a cited author's gender.
- Geography: define locations on a map or in text; "the East Coast **of the
  United States**"; hemisphere-neutral words — poleward/equatorward, not
  northward/southward; cyclonic/anticyclonic vorticity, not
  positive/negative.
- Culture: avoid untranslatable idioms ("throwing out the baby with the
  bathwater").
