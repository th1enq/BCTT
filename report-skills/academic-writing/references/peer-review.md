# Peer Review, Authorship, Ethics, and ESL Guidance

## How the process works (know the game)

Authors → editor (decides) → reviewers (advise only — "reviewer", not
"referee"). Recommendations: accept as is (<1%), minor revisions, major
revisions, reject, transfer. **Editors decide, not reviewers; the author
must satisfy the editor.** Editors weigh review quality, may seek extra
reviews on split verdicts, and can reject mid-process if the author dodges
key concerns, revisions expose deeper flaws, or convergence is too slow.

## Writing a referee report

**Accept the job only if** you can meet the deadline (or negotiate one), you
have the expertise (or declare which parts you can judge), and you're
unbiased (no conflicts, no strong personal stakes). Fair exchange: if you
submit N papers/year, perform 2N–3N reviews. Early-career scientists often
write the most thorough reviews — say yes.

Obligations: confidentiality; respect the authors' right to their own style
in a high-quality manuscript; **never criticize the author personally**.

**Procedure:**
1. Print single-sided; first read purely for comprehension (if you can't
   understand it, the fault may be the author's).
2. Apply the critical-reading template: Does the title/abstract represent
   the work? What is the purpose and hypothesis? What's known / added /
   unknown? Are data and methods adequate and suited to the hypotheses? Are
   interpretations sound; could the evidence read differently? Do figures
   support the claims? Are conclusions complete, not overstated? What are
   the limitations? Is the contribution significant? Are references
   appropriate?
3. Second read: write the review. Third read: confirm. Proofread and
   soften.

**Decision logic:** judge science quality × presentation quality — both must
clear the bar; excellent science presented badly is still rejectable.
Recommend rejection when revisions can't realistically fix it. Don't fear
being harsh: keep criticism scientific and defensible.

**Review structure:** preamble (manuscript title/number, summary of
contribution, positives and negatives, recommendation) → numbered concerns:
fatal flaws → major → minor → typos, each located ("p. 4, line 13"). Group
recurring problems ("these examples are representative, not
comprehensive").

**Tone:** feedback sandwich — criticism between genuine positives. Every
criticism carries evidence, the reason, and a suggested fix; point to places
the author did it *well* as models. Replace "awkward/unclear" with "If I
understand correctly, you meant… ?" Distinguish changes needed for clarity
from matters of taste; review the paper in front of you, not the paper you
wish they'd written (suggest extra analyses; don't mandate them).

Anonymity is the default; reveal yourself only deliberately (to open
dialogue, when self-citation exposes you anyway, or for credit).

**Informal reviews for colleagues:** first ask the deadline and the expected
editing depth; big issues first; teach with one worked paragraph, let the
author apply it to the rest; in-person beats a bleeding markup.

## Responding to reviews

Expect stings; park the reviews for a week before acting (the seven stages:
shock → anger → fear → bewilderment → frustration → acceptance →
happiness). Comment types: (1) agree & easy, (2) hard but important, (3)
taste — adopt or rebut, (4) reviewer misread — first ask *what in my text
allowed the misreading*; what confused one reviewer will confuse readers, so
clarify even while rebutting.

**Response document (Samelson rules):**
- Quote/point to each independent comment; respond point-by-point.
- For each: state the exact revision made, with section/page, quoting new
  text where practical. Avoid "Section X was rewritten" vagueness.
- If you didn't revise: say so and justify. A detailed rebuttal usually
  deserves partial inclusion in the manuscript — other readers will have the
  same question.
- Open with a brief summary of major changes and major refusals.

Attach a cover letter (overview + editor-only issues). Treat the editor as a
colleague; ask for guidance when reviews conflict.

Traps: "return for major revisions" ≠ acceptance — ~13% of AMS rejections
happened *after* initial revision, mostly for token revisions or slow
convergence. Submitting is a privilege; ignoring volunteer reviewers' work
gets manuscripts killed. Never shop an unrevised rejected manuscript to
another journal — communities are small and reviewers recur.

Specific situations:
- Tangential extra analysis requested → agree it's interesting, "beyond the
  scope of the present manuscript" (maybe your next paper).
- Reviewer missed your point → restate it in more places, more plainly.
- Vague hostile review → lead your response with a few strong rebuttals
  citing page/line of the overlooked material, then dismantle item by item;
  stay on the moral high ground; flag tone concerns to the editor in the
  cover letter.
- Divergent reviews usually mean the *purpose and utility were understated*
  — fix the framing, not just the details.

**Rejection:** read the decision letter carefully (is a resubmission
invited?); never berate the editor; revise substantially, then resubmit
(same journal: declare the history and attach responses to the original
reviews — it helps the editor weigh reviewer vs author) or submit elsewhere
after real revision. Grünberg's GMR paper was rejected by PRL, published in
PRB with the original submission date preserved — and won the Nobel Prize
plus the patent priority. Rejection ≠ worthless.

## Authorship

ICMJE: an author must satisfy **all three** — (1) substantial contribution
to conception/design or data/analysis/interpretation, (2) drafting or
critically revising, (3) approving the final version. Funding acquisition,
data collection, or supervision alone do not qualify. No gift or prestige
authorship; demand removal if added without merit.

**Authorship = responsibility for the whole paper**, not just your part —
one author's fraud shadows every coauthor. Ordering models: descending
contribution (most common); lab head last; workers first then alphabetical;
quantitative scoring. For thesis-derived papers written up by the advisor,
keeping the student first avoids the perception of theft. Two meta-rules:
discuss authorship openly and early (senior person initiates), and keep the
group's rules consistent.

Corresponding author owes coauthors: early notice, time to comment, reviews
forwarded, status updates, involvement in responses, managed disagreements,
deadlines, page-proof access, reprints.

## Ethics and misconduct

Misconduct = fabrication, falsification, plagiarism (not honest error or
opinion). Notes:
- Fully described quality control is defensible; undescribed filtering can
  be falsification.
- Images: creating/eliminating data, or upsampling resolution, is
  misconduct; small brightness shifts and clearly bounded composites are OK;
  disclose all processing in the caption. Test: "is the adjusted image still
  an accurate representation of the original data?"
- Plagiarism covers ideas, equations, code, hypotheses, and others'
  scholarship — not just text. Web data/figures need credit. Defenses: start
  from your own ideas; cite the intellectual route; in notes rigidly
  separate quotes / paraphrase / your own thoughts; summarize from
  comprehension, never rewrite the source sentence-by-sentence; quote-mark
  even short borrowed phrases. Emulate style, never copy ("beautiful
  sentences from other studies" is not a defense).
- Self-plagiarism: duplicate publication (banned), salami-slicing (declare
  related papers to the editor), text recycling (methods reuse — tolerated
  but gray), copyright infringement (the publisher owns your published
  words). Conference abstracts → journal papers are fine.
- Conflicts of interest: disclose funding with a stake in the outcome
  (usually in acknowledgments); rule of thumb — if post-publication
  disclosure would embarrass you, disclose pre-publication.

## ESL authors (and their coauthors)

English scientific culture is **low-context: explicit and direct**. Flowery,
digressive, or implicit styles read as flaws. Rejections of ESL manuscripts
usually stem less from grammar than from the deeper items below.

Common weaknesses (check in order of damage):
1. Research formulation: purpose/uniqueness unstated; little new science.
2. Literature: cursory, outdated, no synthesis; no comparison of new results
   with prior work.
3. Presentation: methods underdescribed; experiment motivations unexplained;
   equations without physical interpretation; overconfidence ("models and
   data agree perfectly") or overinterpretation; limitations undiscussed;
   text–figure inconsistencies.
4. Flow: missing transitions; choppy rhythm.
5. Language: journal style, word misuse, articles (a/an/the), tense.

Strategies:
- **Word bank** (Meng): collect admired expressions by category
  (transitions, figure descriptions, comparisons, conclusions); use daily in
  small writing exercises.
- **Literature as writing coach** (Montgomery): pick 15–25 well-written
  recent papers in your area; reread regularly; copy paragraphs by hand to
  feel the construction; insert your own imitation sentences; get a native
  speaker to check the exercises. Emulate, don't copy.
- Write directly in English if you can think in it (drop native-language
  placeholders for stuck phrases); direct translation is slower and produces
  unnatural structure — writing two papers takes longer than one.
- Ask helpers targeted questions (science overall + grammar on one or two
  sections, then apply the lessons yourself); native speakers often know
  "what sounds right" without rules — ask whether a change is clarity or
  taste.
- Maintain the weakness list; use writing centers; consider professional
  editing services (vet them: experience, sample edit, confidentiality,
  what the fee covers).
- Native-speaking coauthors: if your name is on it, fixing the grammar is
  part of the job — or withdraw from the author list. Guide ESL colleagues
  down the funnel: organization first, grammar later.
