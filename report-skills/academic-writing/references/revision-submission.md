# Revision, Citations, and Submission

## The hit-by-the-bus moment

The first complete draft is a milestone (celebrate) — and the point where
your role flips from writer to **editor**. Most weak submissions aren't lazy
science; they skipped the editing phase. Admired authors run dozens of
passes (50 drafts is unremarkable).

## Top-down editing: funnel + CPR

Edit largest scale first (organization → paragraphs → sentences → words) —
polishing sentences in text that gets reorganized is wasted work. At each
level apply **CPR** (Schall):

1. **Concision** — trim unnecessary text.
2. **Precision** — make it exact and unambiguous.
3. **Revision** — sharpen transitions and coherence.

### When you've lost your way

Let it sit days; then label each sentence's topic in the margin, group
themes — misplaced material becomes obvious (the book's worked example: an
abstract whose dataset/size/spatial content each appeared in two scattered
places; regrouping fixed it). Scissors-and-table rearrangement still works.

### Précis — compression drill

Rewrite a passage in the minimum words preserving all principal points and
meaning (a published 292-word passage compressed honestly to 119). Use it on
overlong abstracts and memos.

### A worked editing sequence (from the book's hail-abstract example)

1. Reorganize: same-topic sentences adjacent.
2. Concision: delete content dropped from the paper (abstract must track the
   paper!), delete the unimportant, "spatial and temporal occurrence" →
   "climatology".
3. Precision: unify duplicate terms (severe hail vs large hail — pick one);
   "at least 2 cm" → "2 cm or larger"; "many different methods" → name them
   ("newspaper, storm-spotter, and eyewitness reports"); add "77 years" so
   readers don't do mental math (1930–2006); "smallest" → smallest compared
   to what?; vague "era of advanced technology" → the actual technologies;
   ranges → single representative numbers where honest.
4. Revision: transitions, grammar (due to → because of), cleanup.

## Nearing final

- Set it down for days; edit only when fresh (morning before email, etc.).
- Print it; multiple passes each with one goal (argument, transitions,
  grammar).
- Edit back-to-front page-wise; even sentence-by-sentence backward — breaks
  familiarity and finally gives figures/captions/references attention.
- Read aloud: stumbling points are rewrite points.
- Re-validate the working title and abstract against the finished text.
- Consistency sweep: highlight main points and confirm they appear in
  abstract, introduction, body, and conclusions alike.
- **Personal weakness list:** every writer repeats certain errors
  (it-phrases, that/which, whereas/while…). Keep the list; search the
  manuscript for each item, every time.
- Sneaking suspicion rule: any passage you feel vaguely uneasy about WILL be
  flagged by reviewers. Fix it now.
- Done when: no substantive sentence changes left; ≤ ~1 minor fix per page;
  or you're undoing last round's changes — submit the damn thing.

## Final checks (AMS-derived checklist)

- Title page complete (date, corresponding author).
- Abstract and conclusions cite the most important results and agree.
- Terminology consistent throughout; acronyms defined at first use.
- Every citation in the reference list and vice versa (search "19"/"20"
  through the file, ticking off a printed list).
- References verified against the actual sources (authors, year, pages).
- Sections, figures, tables numbered sequentially; every in-text
  figure/table number points to the right object.
- Spelling/grammar checked (tune the checker: keep subject–verb agreement,
  silence passive-voice nags); pages/lines numbered as required.
- Personal weakness list checked.

**Mind the little things.** "That's the copy editor's job" is wrong — sloppy
manuscripts irritate reviewers into rejection, and little errors correlate
with big scientific ones. A well-prepared manuscript takes ~3 hours to copy
edit; a messy one 7+.

## Feedback before submission

Never submit in a "let the reviewers deal with it" panic. Recruit tough
colleagues (yes-people are useless), non-experts (they catch undefined
jargon), and people who disagree with your conclusions (best flaw-finders).
Tell reviewers what kind of feedback you need ("organization, not grammar").
Clean the draft first — or explicitly flag what's rough. University writing
centers and paid editing services are legitimate options.

## The case for shorter papers

Readers prefer them; reviews are friendlier; they're faster to write; small
publishable units keep your name visible; strict length limits exist (e.g.,
7500 words). "Omit needless words… every word tell" (Strunk & White). Right
length = solid arguments + no tangents + concise precise sentences + no
redundancy. But length itself isn't the metric — some 10-page papers are too
long, some 35-page papers too short.

## Citations and references

**Why cite:** demonstrate scholarship; credit ideas; show your idea's
lineage; distinguish your work; and practically — well-chosen citations are
a *shield*: skeptics must dismantle each source to dismiss you.

**What must be cited** (Schall): quotations/opinions/predictions (quoted or
paraphrased), others' statistics, visuals, theories, case studies,
experimental methods/results, specialized procedures.

**How:**
- Author–date vs numbered systems: journal's choice; author–date is easier
  during revision.
- List parenthetical citations chronologically; explain any exception.
  Split heterogeneous lists by type ("observational studies (…), modeling
  studies (…)").
- **Placement = attribution.** A citation supports exactly the clause it
  follows. End-of-sentence is smoothest but may mis-scope; move it
  mid-sentence when precision demands (the undercatch example: three
  references quantified undercatch, not snow ratios — end placement implied
  the wrong thing).
- One citation after a paragraph's topic sentence covers the paragraph.
- Prefer primary, peer-reviewed sources; cite originals rather than reviews
  (Golden Rule — you'd feel robbed too); "(Sanders 1967, cited in Kessler
  2008)" when the original is unobtainable; gray literature (theses,
  conference preprints, websites) sparingly or as "(personal
  communication)" / "(manuscript submitted to …)".
- Don't over-cite yourself; represent every competing group; one-sided
  reviews get spotted (the neglected authors may be your reviewers).
- Blend sources with your own assertions (Schall): open and close
  paragraphs with your own sentences — bookends around the sources;
  introductions/conclusions mostly your own words; announce citations ("A
  2002 study revealed that…"); prove you're managing the material.
- Direct quotes: sparingly, always with context; verbatim with […] for
  changes, "[sic]" for source errors, page numbers included.
- Syntax details: "Johnson (2001) demonstrated" (drop "The … article/study
  by"); e.g. = incomplete example list, i.e. = that is (comma after both,
  US style); et al. needs its period; repeat the year on each mention; no
  "see" prefix; page ranges with an en dash (pp. 1–45); initials to
  distinguish same-surname-same-year authors.
- Two-pass reference check before submission: (1) every citation ↔ list
  entry both ways, (2) every entry verified against a copy of the source.
- **Read what you cite.** An estimated ~80% of cited papers go unread by the
  citing author. Don't inherit others' misreadings or reference-list typos.

## Page proofs and after

- Proofs are for catching errors (yours and the typesetter's), not
  rewriting. Check against the marked-up accepted manuscript; read
  fresh, on paper; enlist a second reader (one reads aloud, one checks).
- Check: figure placement near citations; captions; sequential numbering;
  headers/pages; equations and symbols; references; current affiliations.
  Answer editor queries; request a second proof after heavy corrections.
- After publication, market the paper: send PDFs to cited/acknowledged
  colleagues; self-archive (2–6× more citations); present it; consider a
  press release for newsworthy results; enter paper awards.
- Perspective: ~50% of papers are never cited; satisfaction must come from
  the work itself.
